A train stops. A payment terminal goes blank. A phone still has a battery, but the network it needs is faltering. On 28 April 2025, the loss of electricity interrupted the ordinary machinery of life across Spain and Portugal.4

Behind those interruptions lay a failure of coordination on a very different timescale. The European investigation describes oscillations, gaps in voltage control and generator disconnections combining into a cascade. The blackout was the outcome of that interaction.2

01 / The essentials

A grid has more than one balance to keep

Producing enough electricity is only part of the job. The system must also maintain the electrical conditions that allow equipment to stay connected.

Frequency · measured in hertz

The rhythm of the system

Europe’s alternating-current grid runs at a nominal 50 cycles per second. Frequency reflects the balance between active power supplied and consumed: lose generation without reducing demand, and frequency tends to fall.

Voltage · measured in volts

The electrical driving force

Voltage must stay within safe limits at different points in the network. Its control depends heavily on reactive power: energy exchanged with electric and magnetic fields in AC equipment. Managing that exchange helps keep voltage in range.

02 / The mechanism

How protection becomes a cascade

Disconnecting equipment can protect it locally while making conditions harder for the network that remains. Follow the simplified sequence below.2, 3

Connection depends on control

Generators and consumers share a network. Alongside supplying energy, the system needs resources that respond to changes in voltage. This is the starting principle, not a claim that all conditions before the blackout were normal.

03 / The interpretation

Resist the single-cause story

Was this simply “too much solar”?

The European final findings identify a combination of control gaps, oscillations, rapid output changes and disconnections. That supports a more demanding question than a technology’s share of generation: which resources could stabilise the system, where were they, and how did they behave?2

Was it a cyberattack?

Spain’s government inquiry ruled out a cyberattack in the operator, control centres and generation facilities it examined. Its explanation was electrical. That finding should take precedence over the speculation that circulated during the outage.3

Had the system run out of inertia?

Inertia slows frequency changes through energy stored in rotating machines. The Spanish system operator said inertia exceeded the European recommendation and did not initiate the event. Voltage control and frequency support are related services, but they are not interchangeable.5

Who was responsible?

A technical causal chain does not, by itself, settle liability. Red Eléctrica’s June account emphasised generators’ compliance; the government examined the adequacy of voltage-control provision too. This page uses the later European findings for the overall explanation, rather than adopting one participant’s defence.5, 3, 2

04 / Switching back on

You cannot restart a country like a lamp

Some generators need an external electricity supply before they can start. “Black-start” plants can begin without one. Spain’s recovery drew on hydroelectric stations with that ability, alongside electricity from France and Morocco. Energised islands expanded through the network.3

The transition’s engineering challenge is to deliver stability alongside energy.

The European panel’s recommendations cover operating practice, monitoring, coordination and regulation. Its conclusion is practical: deployable technology exists to strengthen resilience. The task is to make the different parts of the system work together, under stress as well as in normal conditions.2
